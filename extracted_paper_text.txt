Engineering Trustworthy AI: A Developer Guide
for Empirical Risk Minimization
Diana Pfau and Alexander Jung , Member, IEEE
Abstract—AI systems are increasingly used for critical deci-
sions that transcend all important ﬁelds of private and public life.
These systems often use empiri cal risk minimization (ERM) to
train powerful prediction models such as deep neural networks.
The output of the predictive model runs the risk of unintentional
bias, opacity, and other adverse effects. To ensure the safety of
these systems, it is vital to consider these risks already in the
design stage of the model. The EU acknowledged the potential
sensitivity of the predictions/ decisions made about persons,
which led to the formulation of the Ethics Guidelines for Trust-
worthy AI laying down seven key requirements for trustworthy
AI. So far, the design of ERM-based methods prioritises accuracy
over trustworthiness. This article discusses how key requirements
for trustworthy AI can be translated into design choices for ERM
components. We map the design space of ML systems to the
core objectives of trustworthy AI: fairness, privacy, robustness,
and explainability. Our theory i s instrumental in the design of
trustworthy ML systems that minimize privacy leakage and are
robust against (intentional) perturbations during their operation,
such as disseminating fake news. The operation of trustworthy
ML systems should also be transparent or explainable to its
users. Finally, ML systems must be fair and not discriminate
against speciﬁc user groups. There is an urgent need for a
more holistic approach to ML that includes key requirements for
trustworthy AI.
Impact Statement —AI systems are used to make predictions
and decisions about people in many ﬁelds of public and private
life, ranging from law enforcement to advertisement. It is vital
that AI systems follow minimum requirements for robustness,
safety, transparency, privac y and governance, human agency
and oversight, and nondiscrimination, and do not cause harm
to the persons they make decisions about. As the context in
which AI systems are used changes and there are many different
AI applications, it is important to have a wide variety of
methods available to developers to make sensible design choices
for trustworthy AI. Our contribution consists in proposing and
showcasing different design choices for ERM components to fulﬁll
the requirements of trustworthy AI. This is an important step
to make AI systems safe and socially beneﬁcial and to meet

C. Connecting ERM to Trustworthy AI Requirements
Empirical risk minimization (ERM) is the foundational opti-
mization principle underlying most modern ML systems [24],
[25]. The idea of ERM is intuitive: train a ML model by mini-
mizing the average error (or loss) incurred by its predictions on
a training set. Design choices within ERM, speciﬁcally the con-
struction of the dataset, the selection of the model architecture,
and the error measure (or loss), directly inﬂuence the ability of
AI systems to meet key requirements for trustworthiness (see
Fig. 1. Trustworthy AI adds new design criteria for ERM based methods.
Besides small computational complexity and data requirements, these methods
must be sufﬁciently explainable, priv acy-friendly, fair and robust.
Fig. 1). In this article, we systematically map the design choices
within ERM to the seven key requirements for trustworthy
AI (KR1–KR7) outlined by the European Commission (see
Table I). In the following, we will show that trustworthy AI
can be engineered by rigorously designing ERM components
to align with values such as human agency, technical robust-
ness, privacy, fairness, transparency, societal well-being, and
accountability. Importantly, achieving trustworthy AI goes be-
yond good design choices and requires interdisciplinary teams
of developers, lawyers, ethics specialists, and other experts in
assessing the requirements considering the speciﬁc use-case.

III. D ESIGN CHOICES AND TRUSTWORTHY AI
In the design of an AI application, there are many different
design choices that can be made (see Fig.4). While traditionally
computational and statistical aspects are the most prominent
guiding principles, it is crucial for the further development
of AI to take into account requirements of trustworthiness to
ensure that AI applications are also socially beneﬁcial and save.
The European Union has identiﬁed seven key requirements
for trustworthy artiﬁcial intelligence [20]. Even though these
requirements are not legally bi nding, their ultimate legal basis
lies in the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights and, therefore, in
the EU’s core democratic values. To create trustworthy AI it is
essential to develop an understanding of these norms. Hence, in
the following, we will propose different design choices in the
context of the key requirements and provide more information
about their relationship to core values of the European Union
(see Fig. 5). However, it is important to note that technical
design choices repres ent only one pillar of trustworthy AI.
Especially oftentimes required balancing tests of rights assess-
ments will have to be conducted by legal experts in cooperation
with the AI experts. Additionally , organizational, contractual
and educational factors must be considered to ensure that AI
applications are truly trustworthy.
IV . KR1 - HUMAN AGENCY AND OVERSIGHT
The basic idea of human agency and oversight [ 20, p.15]
relates to human autonomy as the right to live one’s life as
one wishes (liberty) [30, Article 6], and human dignity, the fact
that all persons, by virtue of being human, have an inherent,
equal, and inalienable value regardless of any other factors
[30, Article 1]. Human agency and oversight implement these
values by requiring that ultim ately a person be meaningfully
involved in the process of decision-making; an AI system shall
not be able to make uncontrolled decisions about people. To
ensure feasibility of this oversi ght, AI systems need to pro-
vide for transparency, whic h could be seen as precondition

for agency and oversight. Transparency, however, is a separate
requirement within the guidelines and will be treated separately
in the following.
ERM-based methods must be designed to support user
agency, ensure human oversight, and protect fundamental
rights. The predictions delivered by a trained model must
not result in any manipulation or undue inﬂuence that would
deprive the person subjected to decisions of meaningful de-
cision making. We must ensure safeguards to maintain hu-
man control and to prevent harmful outcomes. KR1 is closely
related to fundamental rights such as dignity, freedom, and
nondiscrimination [30].
A. Human Agency
Users should be able to understand, interact with, and chal-
lenge decisions based on predictionsˆh(x) delivered by a trained
modelˆh∈H . Human agency is facilitated by using transparent
models and comprehensive documentation of the training pro-
cess [e.g. optimization method used to solve ( 3)]. The ERM
design choices for data points (their features and label) and
loss function must ensure that the trained model avoids any
manipulation or deception of users, all of which may threaten
individual autonomy [31], [32], [33].
B. Human Oversight
We must design ERM-based methods that do not compromise
autonomy or cause harm. This can be implemented through
various governance models that allow for varying degrees of
human intervention, from direct involvement in learning cycles
(monitoring gradient bases methods to solve ( 3)) to greater
oversight of the social and ethical impacts resulting from pre-
dictions h(x).
Next, we discuss how KR1 guides the design choice for data
(training set), model and loss used in ERM ( 4).
C. Data
1) Freedom of the Individual: Guaranteeing individual free-
dom requires that individuals, especially those at risk of ex-
clusion, have equal access to the beneﬁts and opportunities
that AI can offer. In this regard, KR1 requires that the train-
ing set in ( 3) is curated with diversity and inclusion in mind.
Biases in data collection or labeling may disproportionately
affect certain groups , potentially limiting their autonomy or
reinforcing discriminatory patterns. Unbiased representation of
subpopulations in the datasetD used by ERM (4) is essential to
avoid manipulation, deception, or discrimination of individuals
and to protect their autonomy and freedom of choice.
2) Respect for Human Dignity: Learning personalized
model parameters for recommender systems allows supplying
customised suggestions to users, referred to as microtargeting.
This can be useful as it can help users to ﬁnd suitable contents or
products. However, microtargeting can also increase addictive
user behavior or even emotional manipulation of larger user
groups [17], [34], [35], [36]. KR1 rules out certain design
choices for the labels of data points to defuse microtargeting. In
particular, we must avoid using the mental and psychological
characteristics of a user as the label. KR1 also rules out loss
functions that can be used to train predictors of psychological
characteristics.
3) Continuous Monitoring: In their simplest form, ERM-
based methods involve a single training phase, i.e. they solve (3)
using some numerical optimisation method [37], [38]. The use
of a single training phase is only useful if the data generation
is stationary, e.g., if it can be well approximated by an i.i.d.
assumption. For many ML applications, this assumption is only
realistic if the training set is limited to a sufﬁciently short
time period [39], [40]. It is crucial to continuously compute a
validation error on a current validation set used to diagnose the
whole ML system (see [24, Sec. 6.6]). Based on the diagnosis,
the model parameters can be updated (retrained) by using a
fresh training set for ERM.
D. Model
Human agency and oversight can be facilitated by relying on
simple models such as linear models with few features or deci-
sion trees with small depth. Deﬁning what makes a model “sim-
ple” is challenging. However, a more rigorous understanding of
model simplicity can be developed through quantitative mea-
sures of their explainability (o r interpretability). Section VII
constructs measures of the subjective explainability of a trained
modelˆh∈H . Generally speaking, we refer to a model as simple
if a nonexpert can (to some degree) understand how the features
of a data point relate to the prediction h(x).
While the internal logic of simple models (such as linear
classiﬁers) may be transparent, malicious actors may repurpose
them in harmful or unintended ways. These attacks on simple
models are difﬁcult to foresee at design time. Thus, while the
use of simple (or interpretable) models supports oversight and
auditing, it must be complemented by broader socio-technical
assessments and usage controls [41], [42].
E. Loss
The choice of the loss function in ERM ( 4) should favour
hypothesis maps h∈H that are consistent with fundamental
rights. For example, including a penalty term in the loss func-
tion can force the trained model to yield predictions that are

even though those woul d not be included in the initial data
collection, it is still possibl e that those could be accidentially
inferred from user behaviour. Wecan also explicitly incorporate
domain expertise of psychologists to penalize predictions that
would recommend harmful content to social media users [43].
1) Interpretable Loss Function: To facilitate human over-
sight, we should use a loss functio n that is intelligible to the
user. For example, consider a user without formal training or
education in ML. Here, using, the 0/1 loss might enable human

V. K R 2- TECHNICAL ROBUSTNESS AND SAFETY
ERM-based ML methods should work reliably even when
things go wrong. They must be safe in real-world conditions
where data might be messy, models might be slightly wrong,
and computing systems might fail. This is the idea behind Key
Requirement 2 (KR2) [20, p.16], which supports important
rights in the EU Charter such as the right to life [30, Article 2],
protection of health [ 30, Article 3], and protection of personal
data [30, Article 8].
In this section, we look at what can go wrong in ERM-based
systems and how to design them to stay safe and reliable.
Where Do Problems Come From?To solve an ERM problem
like ( 3), we run optimization algorithms on real computers.
These computers are not perfect [44], [45]
1) The code might have bugs.
2) Hardware can crash or lose power.
3) Communication links might fail.
4) There is always some rounding error in ﬂoating-point
calculations.
Even if the computation works ﬁne, the training data D might
be noisy or tampered with
1) Sensors deliver noisy measurements [46].
2) Attackers can inject fake or misleading data [47], [48].
In practice, the data used for training is typically a random
sample drawn from an unknown underlying distribution [24],
[25]. As a result, each training set may differ slightly. A robust
model should maintain strong performance despite such vari-
ability in the training data.
A. Stability Helps Generalization
A central concept in learning theory is that if a learning
algorithm exhibits only minor changes in its output when the
training set is perturbed slightly, it is likely to generalize well
to unseen data [49], [50], [51]. In other words, stable methods
often perform well on new, unseen data.
While many forms of data variation are small and random,
real-world datasets often contain more severe forms of contam-
ination. In particular, the presence of even a small number of
extreme outliers—arising from s ensor malfunctions, data entry
errors, or adversarial manipulation—can severely compromise
the reliability of a learning algorithm. This vulnerability poses
a critical challenge in safety-sensitive domains such as medical
diagnostics or autonomous systems.
Robust statistics offers a principled framework for address-
ing this issue. A key concept in this ﬁeld is the breakdown
point, which serves as a formal robustness metric for statistical
estimators. Speciﬁcally, the breakdown point quantiﬁes the
largest proportion of contaminated data an estimator can tol-
erate before it yields arbitrarily incorrect results. For example.
1) The sample mean has a breakdown point of zero; a single
outlier can render it invalid.
2) The sample median, by contrast, has a breakdown point
of 50%, making it substantially more robust [52].
What Can Go Wrong? Robustness means the model still
performs well when things go slightly wrong. These problems
can happen in three places.
1) Data: Noise, adversarial samples, or changes in the data
distribution.
2) Model: Changes in architecture, unstable parameters, or
software updates.
3) Loss Function: Poorly chosen objective, or adversarial
manipulation of the loss.

model class, and loss function

VI. KR3 - PRIVACY AND DATA GOVERNANCE
ERM-based methods must ensure protection against unau-
thorised access to and misuse of personal data. Such protection
is critical in the face of concrete threats such as membership
inference attacks, which attempt to infer whether a particular
individual’s data were used in training [70]. Data protection
and privacy protection are typically implemented as part of a
broader data governance framework [71], designed to ensure
the quality, integrity, and lawful processing of personal data.
A. Data Governance
Many applications of ERM use data points that represent
individuals, often capturing behavioral patterns, preferences,
or sensitive medical information. Especially for such personal
data, data governance must cover the entire AI lifecycle, from
collection and training to model deployment and secure dele-
tion. Effective governance includes data lineage, model vali-
dation (e.g., verifying factual accuracy), and documenting data
sources [72].
Legal frameworks such as the General Data Protection Reg-
ulation mandate safeguards such as data protection impact as-
sessments (DPIAs) and, in some cases, the appointment of a
Data Protection Ofﬁcer. These mechanisms help anticipate and
mitigate privacy risks throughout the system’s operation [73].
1) Measuring Privacy Leakage: A fundamental question is
how much private information can leak from the output of an
ERM-based system. Formally, we can model such a system as a
function A that maps a training set D to an output A(D), such
as the trained parameters ˆw or a prediction ˆh(x). Membership
inference attacksexploit this map to determine whether a partic-
ular data point was used during training—potentially violating
privacy even if the model output seems innocuous.
2) Privacy Protection and Noninvertibility: To prevent such
leakage, it must be computationally infeasible to infer sensitive
components of any data point from A(D). Let each data point
be represented by features x, a label y, and a sensitive attribute
s (e.g., a medical diagnosis).
Privacy Protection that the map A be sufﬁciently
noninvertible—meaning not just that A(D) lacks an inverse
(in a strict mathematical sense), but that even sophisticated
inference attacks cannot reliably reconstruct s or deduce the
presence of a speciﬁc data point. This notion is tightly linked
to Plausible Seniability : a property whereby the inclusion of
a speciﬁc data point in D cannot be conﬁrmed or denied with
high conﬁdence based on the observed output.
3) Differential Privacy (DP): A widely used approach to
make an ERM-based method sufﬁciently noninvertible is to
introduce some randomness or noise. Examples of such ran-
domness include adding noise to the output and selecting a
random subset of D.T h em a pA then becomes stochastic and,
in turn, the output A(D) is then characterized by a probability

B. Data
A simple way to implement privacy protection in ERM-
based methods is through careful selection of the features used
to characterize data points [77], [78]. The goal is to select
features that are relevant to the learning task while ensuring they
do not reveal excessive information about sensitive attributes.
Note that it is typically not sufﬁcient to just remove sensitive
attributes from raw data points to ensure privacy protection.
Indeed, these sensitive attributes might be recoverable from the
other features of a data point.

A straightforward approach to implementing privacy protec-
tion within ERM-based methods involves the deliberate selec-
tion of features used to characterize each data point [77], [78].
The aim is to retain only those features that are relevant for the
learning task, while minimizing the risk of disclosing sensitive
information. However, it is typically insufﬁcient to ensure pri-
vacy protection by merely excluding explicit sensitive attributes
from the input data. This is because such attributes can often be
inferred indirectly through other correlated features—known as
proxy variables—that act as functional or statistical surrogates.
Therefore, effective privacy protection requires a more holistic
view that accounts for the interactions between features, and
may involve additional techniques such as adversarial training,
regularization, or differential privacy to mitigate leakage from
correlated attributes.
There is an inherent trade-off between privacy protection and
the resulting statistical accuracy.In fact, we can trivially achieve
perfect privacy protection by not using any property of a data
point as a features. However, this extreme case of maximum
privacy protection comes at the cost of a lower quality of the
predictions delivered by (the hypothesis learnt from) ERM.

statistic [81].

A. Traceability
The design choices (and underlying business models) for

B. Explainability
Explainability is a central pillar of trustworthy AI, grounded
in the principle that individuals should be able to understand the
decisions made about them, particularly so they can respond or
seek redress in cases of unfair treatment. Various explainabil-
ity techniques have been proposed in the literature, including
LIME [86] and SHAP[87]. LIME approximates a model locally
around one instance with a simple model and shows which
features contributed most to that instance’s prediction. SHAP
on the other hand computes a contribution score (SHAP value)
for each feature of each instance, based on how much it changes
the model output when included vs excluded in all possible
combinations. One possible downside of both approaches could
still be the intelligibility of suchfound explanations for the data
subject.
The recent court case C-203/22 [88] also highlighted the
potential of counterfactual explanations to clarify automated
decision-making. Counterfactual explanations were discussed
by legal scholars early on[89] and can provide a clear picture of
what would need to change in the circumstances of the person in
order for a decision made about the person to change. However,
there is no universally accepted metric for explanaibility so far
and it is more than likely that several approaches can be used
depending on the individual scenario.
Our proposed explainability meas ure differs from these ap-
proaches in so far as it provides a quantiﬁable metric for ex-
plainability. This makes it especially valuable in domains such

A. Data
The training set D used in ERM should be carefully chosen
so as not to reinforce existing discrimination. In a healthcare
application, there may be signiﬁcantly more training data for
patients of a particular gender, resulting in models that perform
best for that gender at the expense of poorer performance for
the minority [75, Sec. 3.3.].
Fairness is also important for ML methods used to determine
creditworthiness and, therefore, whether or not a loan should
be granted [111]. Here, we need to ensure that ML methods do
not discriminate against customers on the basis of ethnicity or
race. To do this, we could augment data points by modifying
any features that primarily reﬂect a customer‘s ethnicity or race
(see Fig. 12).
Data augmentation to enforce the fairness of the ERM is
also studied in [112]. The data augmentation strategy in that
article involves replacing individuals in video frames with new
individuals while preserving the original motion [113], [114].
The two-step process involves: 1) tracking and segmenting the
target person in the video; and 2) replacing the person with
another individual by transforming key-points and poses.
Fair Data Collection: The fairness of ERM-based methods
includes the data collection process. The data points used in
the training set D of ERM ( 4) must be collected in a way
that is compliant with fundamental rights and regulations such
as the General Data Protection Regulation [30], [115].D a t a
collection should be transparent and representative, avoiding
biased sampling and improper consent procedures[116], [117].
B. Model
We can ensure the fairness of the learnt hypothesis ˆh∈H
by using a model H that contains only hypothesis maps sat-
isfying fairness constraints such as (variations) of ( 22). One
example for such a constraint is to require each hypothesis h
to be Lipschitz continuous [101]. The idea is to require ˆh to
deliver similar predictions for data points that are similar in
a nondiscriminatory sense. A key challenge for the practical
use of this requirement is to ﬁnd a useful choice for the metric
underlying the Lipschitz condition [101].
C. Loss
1) Fairness Through Regularization: Fairness constraints
of the form ( 23) can be included in the loss of ERM. Using
Lagrangian duality [ 37, Ch. 5], the constraints can be trans-
lated into a penalty term which is added to the ERM objective
function [105], [107], [118], [119]. Adding such a fairness
penalty term can be interpreted as a form of regularization (see
Section II and Fig. 4).
2) Fairness via Sample Weighting: Instead of adding a
penalty term to the loss function in ERM, we can also ensure
fairness through sample weighting [120]. The rationale is to
scale the loss incurred on a data point based on the relative
frequency of its sensitive attribute in the training setD. Magni-
fying the loss incurred for data points from a minority ensures
that underrepresented groups have a greater inﬂuence on the
solution of (4).
IX. KR6 - SOCIETAL AND ENVIRONMENTAL WELL-BEING
KR6 [20, p.19] covers the impact of AI systems on environ-
mental and social well-being [121],[ 30, Article 35]. The EU
Charter of Fundamental rights in Article 37 mandates a duty of
the EU to provide for a high level of environmental protection
and commitment to sustainable development[30]. This require-
ment therefore guides AI development to take environmental
factors into account and thereby supports individual rights and
collective welfare.
So far, we have discussed KRs that focus on the impact
of ERM-based methods on individual users. In contrast, KR6
revolves around the broader impact of an ERM-based method
at the societal and natural environment level.
A. Society and Democracy
Design choices for ERM should also consider the impact of
(predictions delivered by) a trained model ˆh∈H on society as
a whole. The predictions ˆh(x) could not only be detrimental

to the mental health of individual users but also affect core
democratic processes such as policy-making or elections. For
example, social media apps train personalized models ˆh to rec-
ommend (or select) content delivered to its users. The resulting
tailored ﬁltering of content can boost polarisation and, in the
extreme case, social unrest [122].
B. Environment
ERM-based AI systems need to solve the optimisation prob-
lem ( 3) using some computational methods. Implementing
these methods in physical hardware requires energy, which is
typically provided in the form of electricity [123].G i v e nt h e
increasing energy demand of AI systems, it is crucial to use
environmentally friendly means of energy production [124].
Design choices for ERM should minimize the energy and cool-
ing water requirements [125], of the resulting AI system. These
requirements depend not only on the computational effort re-
quired to solve ERM (4) but also on the choice of data collection
strategies [126].
X. KR7 - ACCOUNTABILITY
KR7 [20, p.19] supplements the other key requirements and
is closely related to the key requirement of fairness (KR5).
Accountability requires mechanism s to identify, explain, and
address the potential harms of AI systems. Organizations op-
erating an AI system are responsible for its direct and indirect
effects on the user [127], [128]. Developers and deployers must
take steps to explain the objectives, motivations, and reasons
for the behaviour and outcome o f AI systems. Accountability
includes the reporting of data b reaches and the possibility of
redress [129].
A. Policy and Governance Approaches
Organizations such as the OECD have been working on gov-
ernance structures for ERM-based AI systems. This includes
formalising auditing procedures and ensuring that developers
adhere to both ethical standards and legal requirements [130].
B. Frameworks for Answerability
AI developers and operators need to be able to justify their
actions and decisions. This includes both transparency and over-
sight mechanisms, which are particularly important in high-
stakes domains [131]. The justiﬁcation of ERM design choices
also requires a solid understanding of the inherent trade-offs
between design criteria such as explainability and accuracy
[95], [132].
C. Regular Audits and Third-Party Reviews
Periodic reviews of AI systems by independent auditors help
ensure and validate account ability. To this end, independent
external teams (“red teams”) should stress-test the ERM-based
system for vulnerabilities and biases that could undermine ac-
countability [130], [133].
Overall, accountability requires a good understanding of the
entire design process of the AI system, as well as proper doc-
umentation and evaluation of the trade-offs during the design
process and therefore can only be achieved if the other key
requirements are taken seriously and well documented.
XI. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
It is essential for the future development of AI systems that
research focuses on the creating trustworthy AI to ensure that
any development is beneﬁcial to individuals and society at
large and is consistent with sustainability goals and democratic
processes. AI systems pose an imminent risk of being harmful,
biased, and opaque. To prevent negative impacts from the use of
AI systems, it is also necessary to understand the core values we
seek to protect to safeguard individuals and societies. Therefore,
we structured our work by ﬁrst providing an overview over core
principles of trustworthy AI and their basis in European fun-
damental rights and second proposing different design choices
that would allow us to realise those principles.
We proposed different design choices using ERM methods
to realise the requirements of human agency and oversight,
technical robustness and safety, privacy and data governance,
transparency, diversity, nondiscrimination and fairness, societal
and environmental well-being, and accountability.
Future work is needed to better understand potential trade-
offs between these requirements and possible ways to mitigate
their impact. While our contribution is to explore and propose
different ways to address the key requirements proposed by the
EU, more research is needed to gain a clearer understanding
of speciﬁc use cases and potential harms that can be mitigated
through design choices in the process of planning and building
AI systems. Finally, more interdisciplinary research will be
needed to examine the implementation of AI legislation and its
limits.
